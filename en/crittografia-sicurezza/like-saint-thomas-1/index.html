I’m starting this new article by saying that I needed to write it for reasons that will be clear later on (I mean in terms of months). But that doesn’t take away from the fact that what we’re about to see is truly cool. Long story short? Suppose you have to perform mathematical operations on sensitive data. I don’t know! You want, for example, to compute the average age of patients in a hospital, but for privacy reasons you don’t have the data in plaintext. Well, with what I’m going to show you, you’ll be able to do all (or almost) the operations you want, while keeping the data encrypted. In other words, you don’t need to decrypt your data to work on it. Maximum privacy within everyone’s reach (sure, even if then on Facebook they tell us when and how they took a dump but oh well, let’s draw a merciful veil).

Homomorphism Explained To My Daughter

Let’s start with the fact that the term homomorphism is not a term used to indicate an irrational aversion towards something and/or someone based on prejudice and/or stereotypes. It’s something a little more useful than unmotivated hatred. Homomorphism is a concept of abstract algebra and indicates a function between two groups that keeps the result of operations unchanged. The two groups \(A\) and \(B\) are not picked at random, but must be chosen so that, given a function

\[ \phi: A \to B \]

the operations "behave well" (yes I know, in a moment we’ll see what that means). So, once constructed, we have a homomorphism if given an operation \(f\) we have that:

  • Performing the operation \(f\) on \(A\) obtaining \(f_A\) and then applying \(\phi\) to it

or

  • Applying \(\phi\) to the elements of \(A\) obtaining \(B\) and performing the operation \(f\) on \(B\) obtaining \(f_B\)

returns the same result.

So \(\phi\) lets us do computations indifferently in \(A\) or \(B\) because the result is equivalent, that is, we can write:

\[ \phi (f_A (x_1, \, x_2, \, \dots , \, x_n) ) = f_B ( \phi(x_1), \, \phi(x_2), \, \dots , \, \phi(x_n)) \]

Formula 1. Homomorphism

Ah, in all of this I forgot to tell you what a group is. Nothing simpler:

A group is a set equipped with an internal operation, that is, such that, given two elements of the set, it returns again an element of the same set.

This operation must satisfy three properties:

  1. It must be associative.
  2. It must admit an identity element.
  3. Every element must have an inverse with respect to it.

In other words, the group is a structure in which the operation always stays within the set. For example, the set of integers with addition

\[ (\mathbb{Z}, +) \]

is a group. In fact

  1. Adding two integers we obtain again an integer.
  2. \(0\) is the identity element of addition.
  3. Every number \(n\) has \(-n\) as its inverse.

Be careful though, \(\mathbb{Z}\) is not automatically a group with any operation. For example

\[ (\mathbb{Z}, \cdot) \]

is not a group, because not all elements have a multiplicative inverse in \(\mathbb{Z}\) (or rather, only the elements \(1\) and \(-1\) do). In fact, the inverse of \(2\) would be \(\frac12\), which does not belong to \(\mathbb{Z}\).

But let’s go further and consider a ring, which is an algebraic structure with two operations, typically addition and multiplication. For now we’re not interested in getting into the details, it’s enough to know that compared to a group two operations appear instead of one. But back to us and let’s continue with an example in order to clarify everything. Consider the addition operation and take as groups:

  1. The set of integers \(\to (\mathbb{Z}, +)\)
  2. The set of numbers modulo \(5 \to (\mathbb{Z}_5, +)\)

A function that allows the passage

\[ (\mathbb{Z}, \, +) \to (\mathbb{Z}_5, \, +) \]

is the following:

\[ \phi(x) = x \pmod{5} \]

(if modular arithmetic is not familiar to you, you can read this article of mine). Since the operation is addition, given two numbers \(a\) and \(b\), to have a homomorphism it must turn out to be true that:

\[ \phi(a + b) = \phi(a) + \phi(b) \]

Let’s verify it by taking \(a=7\) and \(b=4\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \phi(a + b) &= (7 + 4) \pmod{5} \\ &= 11 \pmod{5} \\ & \equiv 1 \pmod{5} \\[6pt] \phi(a) + \phi(b) &= 7 \pmod{5} + 4 \pmod{5} \\ &= 2 \pmod{5} + 4 \pmod{5} \\ &= 6 \pmod{5} \\ & \equiv 1 \pmod{5} \end{aligned} \]

Since the result is the same, and this can be shown for any value of \(a\) and \(b\), we have that \(\phi\) is a homomorphism between the groups \((\mathbb{Z}, +)\) and \((\mathbb{Z}_5, +)\). In this example for simplicity I considered only addition, but it can be shown that it also holds for multiplication:

\[ \phi(a \cdot b) = \phi(a) \cdot \phi(b) \]

So \(\phi\) is a homomorphism between the rings \((\mathbb{Z}, +, \cdot)\) and \((\mathbb{Z}_5, +, \cdot)\). Since subtraction is defined starting from addition, it too is compatible with \(\phi\). But not all that glitters is gold, and we conclude this paragraph by saying that it’s pointless to consider division because, as shown before, it does not define an internal operation on these sets. And since \((\mathbb{Z}, \, \div)\) is not a group it doesn’t even make sense to talk about homomorphism with respect to division.

Seeing Is Believing

This article is not titled "Like Saint Thomas" by chance. As anticipated in the introduction we can exploit homomorphism in a very interesting way. Since doing computations in \(A\) or \(B\) is equivalent, what prevents us from applying this principle to do computations on encrypted data? This would allow us to make transformations on data without having to decrypt it, and thus preserving privacy. Yes I know, just like Saint Thomas, if you don’t see it you don’t believe it. And so the purpose of this article is to show you homomorphic encryption (in a less sadistic way than Saint Thomas, obviously). A small reinterpretation of what we have talked about is enough:

  • \(A\) is the set of plaintext data
  • \(B\) is the set of encrypted data
  • \(f_{A, B}\) is the operation that we want to perform on plaintext data and on encrypted data respectively
  • \(\phi\) is the encryption function \(Enc\)

What we want to do is:

\[ Enc \big( f_A (x_1, \, x_2, \, \dots , \, x_n) \big) = f_B \big( Enc(x_1), \, Enc(x_2), \, \dots , \, Enc(x_n) \big) \]

and consequently

\[ Dec \Big( Enc \big( f_A (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) \big) \Big) = Dec \Big( f_B \big( Enc (x_1), Enc (x_2), \dots, Enc (x_n) \big) \Big) \\[6pt] \Rightarrow f_A (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) = Dec \Big( f_B \big( Enc (x_1), Enc (x_2), \dots, Enc (x_n) \big) \Big) \]

that is, we can do computations before or after encrypting the data, obtaining the same results. This means having the possibility to use a service to process our encrypted data obtaining a result (which we will decrypt to get the plaintext one) without ever actually providing any information. I point out that we keep the notation \(f_A\) and \(f_B\) because it is not said that the operations on plaintext data and on encrypted data are the same. The only thing required is that once the ciphertext obtained after performing the operation \(f_B\) is decrypted, the result is the same as the one obtained by applying \(f_A\) in plaintext. In any case, let’s write down the shopping list of what we need:

  • \(KeyGen()\) is a function that generates a key pair, a public \(pk\) and a private \(sk\) for encryption and decryption. For simplicity, let’s just say that it generates a key \(k\).
  • \(Enc_k(m)\) is a function that takes as input a message \(m\) and a key \(k\) and returns an encrypted message \(c\).
  • \(Dec_k(c)\) is a function that takes as input an encrypted message \(c\) and a key \(k\) and returns a plaintext message \(m\).
  • \(Eval(f, \, (c_1, \, \dots, \, c_n))\) is a function that applies the operation \(f\) to a set of encrypted messages \(c_1, \, \dots, \, c_n\) returning an encrypted result \(c'\).

Suppose we use the famous and extremely sophisticated service "Bullshit Meter" which is able to tell you how stupid a statement made by a person is based on the person’s age, frequency of statements and number of previous contradictions. And suppose we want to establish how stupid the statement

I want Iceland (confusing it with Greenland, but oh well, what are we even talking about...)

is. Let’s start by defining the variables we need:

  • \(x\) = age of the person \(\to\) a lot
  • \(y\) = frequency of statements \(\to\) too many
  • \(z\) = number of contradictions \(\to\) many

since we cannot send this data in plaintext because the special forces AIS are always lurking, we encrypt it and send it to the service. Since we are the only owners of the key we know that the server will not be able to decrypt the data, but it will still be able to perform the computations. Let’s imagine that the computation to establish how stupid a statement is is very heavy (that’s why we have an external service do it) and that it is given by the following formula:

\[ f(x, \, y, \, z) = 2 \cdot x \cdot y + z \]

As you can see, we have delegated the computations to the service which works only on encrypted elements. We just take the result and decrypt it to have the plaintext result. In this way we were able to leverage the computing power of the service without having to provide any sensitive information. Could we have performed the computations ourselves?! Of course. But maybe we don’t have the necessary computing power, or maybe we don’t even know which operations to perform on the data.

Not All Homomorphisms Come Out With A Hole

So far we have seen that, if we have a homomorphism, we can exploit it to do computations on encrypted data. But as we know, nice things end. And doing computations on encrypted data is a nice thing. We can’t do exactly whatever we want with this data.

Let’s start by saying that there are different schemes of homomorphic encryption. Each of these schemes allows executing a certain set of operations, some allow only addition operations, others only multiplication operations, and so on. Homomorphic schemes are, in general, divided into three categories. Let’s see them in more detail.

Partially Homomorphic Encryption

PHE schemes, that is, partially homomorphic ones, allow performing only one type of operation on encrypted data. In practical terms these schemes support either only additions or only multiplications, but not both. Think of it as the uncertainty principle of homomorphic encryption. Without going too much into detail (otherwise I’d have to explain how it works) a very famous PHE scheme is the RSA algorithm which supports multiplication operations (at least in its basic form). For fairness, it seems right to mention at least one PHE scheme that supports addition, and in this case noteworthy (for didactic purposes) is the Benaloh algorithm.

Somewhat Homomorphic Encryption

SHE schemes try a bit harder than PHE. They allow performing both additions and multiplications, and we like this because we can do somewhat more complex operations on encrypted data. But there’s a but..., we cannot perform an arbitrary number of operations. In fact, after a certain number of operations, the ciphertext stops being decryptable correctly. We’ll see why. A widely used variant of this scheme is the LHE scheme, Levelled Homomorphic Encryption. It consists in establishing a priori how many operations we want to perform on encrypted data, and choosing the scheme parameters accordingly. Just to give you a name, an algorithm of this type is BGN (Boneh-Goh-Nissim).

Fully Homomorphic Encryption

The last type of scheme is FHE. By strict logic you should have understood where I’m going with this, right!? It’s the most powerful. It’s the one able to perform all types of operations (or rather all those expressible as sums and products) and in an arbitrary number. Sounds too good to be true, right?! And indeed it is. To obtain this result you pay an enormous cost in terms of both computation and memory. But what do you want to tell you, if you want cool stuff you have to pay for it, otherwise buy it on Amazon Haul. In any case, in this family we have many interesting schemes. Starting from BGV (Brakerski-Gentry-Vaikuntanathan) and BFV (Brakerski-Fan-Vercauteren) which are exact schemes, that is, they work with integers. But we also have approximate schemes like CKKS (Cheon-Kim-Kim-Song) which work with real numbers.

Noise, Noise Everywhere

This is the last paragraph before the conclusions, and its purpose is to not leave you with doubts. Because I know you’re dying to know how all this works. Homomorphism is clear (at least I hope, otherwise your problem), but how do we get to encryption? The secret is in a little thing that can be white and that gives you cheerfulness. Yes indeed, it’s noise. Noise (which can be white) in general is not desired, but in this case it gives us a lot of joy.

This is not meant to be an overly detailed explanation, because in the next article we’ll see an implementation and there many things will be clearer. So let’s start by saying that not all homomorphic schemes are based on noise. Just think about RSA that I mentioned here. In the RSA algorithm there is no noise component necessary for it to function. On the contrary, modern schemes all make use of this so-called noise. Let’s see how...

Schemes like BFV, BGV or CKKS are based on mathematical problems called LWE (Learning With Errors) or RLWE (Ring Learning With Errors). The idea behind LWE is very simple. Suppose we have a system of linear equations of the type

\[ A \cdot s = b \]

Formula 2. Generic Linear System

where \(A\) is a matrix, \(s\) is a vector containing our secret and \(b\) is a vector of constants. Finding \(s\) is quite simple. For example with

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \quad s = \begin{bmatrix}s_1 \\ s_2 \end{bmatrix} \quad b = \begin{bmatrix}8 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix} \]

we would have

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 2s_1 + s_2 &= 8 \\ s_1 + 3s_2 &= 9 \end{aligned} \right. \]

from which we get \(s_1 = 3\) and \(s_2 = 2\) very immediately. But what happens if we added a variable

\[ e = \begin{bmatrix} e_1 \\ e_2 \end{bmatrix} \]

We would have

\[ A \cdot s + e = b \]

from which

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 2s_1 + s_2 + e_1 &= 8 \\ s_1 + 3s_2 + e_2 &= 9 \end{aligned} \right. \]

Now we have a problem. While with two equations and two unknowns the problem was solvable, with the addition of the component \(e\) (the noise, chosen randomly but sufficiently small) the problem is no longer solvable in a unique way. So the component \(s\) cannot be made explicit, fortunately I would add because if it’s a secret it must remain so. LWE consists precisely in this, that is, working with systems of equations with a noise component and, to not miss anything, in modular arithmetic modulo \(q\). So the previous system we write as

\[ A \cdot s + e = b \pmod q \]

Formula 3. LWE System

The point is that you don’t have to solve the system of equations, but only hide the secret. As we’ll see, whoever has the key \(sk\) will still be able to recover the message but this is true as long as the noise remains small, and it is exactly here that the problem of homomorphic encryption is born. Each operation performed on encrypted data, since the noise is part of the system, increases its impact. After too many operations, it becomes so large that it no longer allows recovering the original message correctly. This is why SHE schemes have a limit on the number of operations that can be performed, while FHE schemes must use additional techniques, such as bootstrapping (we’ll see this too), to manage the accumulated noise.

We conclude this paragraph by stating the difference between LWE and RLWE. While LWE is based on systems of linear equations with noise, RLWE extends this concept to more complex algebraic structures, such as rings, yes those very rings I talked about here. In RLWE the system remains of the type

\[ A \cdot s + e = b \pmod q \]

but this time \(A\), \(s\), \(e\) and \(b\) are elements of a polynomial ring, so the "extended" version of the system is written like this:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} a_1(x) \cdot s(x) + e_1(x) &= b_1(x) \pmod q \\ a_2(x) \cdot s(x) + e_2(x) &= b_2(x) \pmod q \\ &\vdots \\ a_n(x) \cdot s(x) + e_n(x) &= b_n(x) \pmod q \end{aligned} \right. \]

Formula 4. RLWE System

where

  • \(a_i(x)\), \(b_i(x)\), \(e_i(x)\) are polynomials with coefficients in \(\mathbb{Z}_q\) (that is, integers modulo \(q\))
  • \(s(x)\) is the secret polynomial with coefficients in \(\mathbb{Z}_q\) (I recommend reading here)
  • \(q\) is the modulus with respect to which the coefficients of the polynomials are reduced

and all operations are performed within a ring of the type

\[ R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(f(x)) \]

with \(f(x)\) a suitably chosen polynomial, usually of the form \(x^n + 1\) (yes, just like in cyclic codes). This means that the polynomials cannot grow indefinitely in degree, but are always reduced with respect to \(f(x)\). This algebraic structure makes it possible to obtain homomorphic schemes that are much more efficient and scalable than the classic LWE case.
