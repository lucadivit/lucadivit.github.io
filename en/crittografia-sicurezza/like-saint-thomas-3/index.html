Size Matters

Let us start by addressing the problem of the size (of \(s\)) that we introduced here. I know, it nags at men, but let us make peace with it. I know it sounds like a cliche, but it is not the size of \(q\) that matters, rather how much you can encrypt. Okay, that was not quite right, but you get the point. In any case, the size problem is a real problem and we cannot pretend it is not there. Let us take two numbers \(a\) and \(b\) and turn them into integers using this formula. We have:

\[ \tilde{a} \approx a \cdot s, \quad \tilde{b} \approx b \cdot s \]

from which:

\[ \tilde{a} + \tilde{b} \approx (a + b) \cdot s \]

and

\[ \tilde{a} \cdot \tilde{b} \approx a \cdot s \cdot b \cdot s = a \cdot b \cdot s^2 \]

While for addition we continue to work with scale \(s\), for multiplication we find ourselves working at scale \(s^2\). And this is a problem. Because, as mentioned last time, there is headroom to respect. If our \(s\) becomes too large, our \(q\) must become even larger and this causes security problems (the reason why, we will see later). And that will not do, the last thing we need is an encryption algorithm that does not protect a thing. So we have to find a way to keep the size of our \(s\) under control.

To bring the scale back under control, we must eliminate one of the two factors \(s\) that appeared during multiplication. The most immediate idea is to divide the result by \(s\). In this way, we obtain a new integer representation of the same value, once again encoded with scale \(s\). Let us look at an example:

\[ a=1.2,\qquad b=0.5,\qquad s=10 \\[6pt] \tilde{a}=12,\qquad \tilde{b}=5 \\[6pt] \tilde{a} \cdot \tilde{b}= 12 \cdot 5 = 60 \]

We are now still at scale \(s^2 = 100\), so we must bring the result back to scale \(s\). To do so, we divide by \(s\):

\[ \frac{\tilde{a} \cdot \tilde{b}}{s} = \frac{60}{10} = 6 \]

We are back at scale \(s=10\) and can decode the result as follows:

\[ \frac{6}{10}=0.6 \]

which corresponds to the correct result of the multiplication \(1.2 \cdot 0.5 = 0.6\). This operation is called rescaling. Its purpose is to reduce the scale after a multiplication, preventing it from growing uncontrollably. Obviously, it would have been too good if simple division were enough. In real CKKS, rescaling is applied directly to the ciphertext and also entails a change to the modulus. But to get there, we must first build something to encrypt and, above all, something to encrypt it with.

Let Us Resize

We said that in CKKS rescaling is not limited to dividing the result by the scale. Along with the scale, the modulus in which the ciphertext coefficients live is also reduced. Let us suppose we start from a ciphertext defined in the ring:

\[ R_q=\mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(x^N+1) \]

After multiplication, its scale has become \(s^2\). To bring it back to a value close to \(s\), we choose a factor \(p\) approximately equal to the scale \(p\approx s\) and divide the ciphertext components by \(p\), rounding the result:

\[ \left\lfloor\frac{ciphertext}{p}\right\rceil \]

Formula 1. Ciphertext Rescaling

The new scale thus becomes \(\frac{s^2}{p}\approx s\). However, by dividing the ciphertext components by \(p\), the result is no longer naturally defined modulo \(q\), but modulo \(\frac{q}{p}\). For this reason, together with the scale, the modulus is also reduced:

\[ q' = \frac{q}{p} \]

Formula 2. Modulus Rescaling

and thus the ciphertext no longer lives in the ring \(R_q\), but in a new

\[ R_{q'} = \mathbb{Z}_{q'}[x]/(x^N+1) \]

We have not therefore recovered for free the space we were consuming. We have eliminated part of it. The ciphertext has returned to a manageable scale, but now it lives inside a smaller enclosure. Let us see why, by dividing the ciphertext, we must also reduce the modulus. I will not explain modular arithmetic again (you can read something here), so let us start from this congruence:

\[ 60 \equiv 1\,060 \pmod{1\,000} \]

The two values represent the same element modulo \(q=1\,000\). Let us now suppose we want to apply rescaling by dividing by \(10\). We obtain: \(\frac{60}{10}=6\) and \(\frac{1\,060}{10}=106\). The problem is that \(6\) and \(106\) are not equivalent modulo \(1\,000\):

\[ 6 \not\equiv 106 \pmod{1\,000} \]

Division is therefore not well-defined if we continue to work with the same modulus. Let us see what happens if we scale the modulus as well. So we move from \(q=1\,000\) to \(q'=\frac{1\,000}{10}=100\), and see what happens:

\[ 6 \equiv 106 \pmod{100} \]

This time the congruence is preserved. This small example shows why rescaling also affects the modulus. The rescaling operation is quite simple from a programming standpoint; in fact, it is just a modular division (rounded) as shown in the following snippet:

def _divide_and_round(value: int, divisor: int) -> int:
    if divisor <= 0:
        raise RescalingError("Il fattore di rescaling deve essere positivo")

    quotient, remainder = divmod(abs(value), divisor)

    if remainder * 2 >= divisor:
        quotient += 1

    if value < 0:
        return -quotient

    return quotient

print(_divide_and_round(value=12, divisor=10)) # Output: 1
print(_divide_and_round(value=16, divisor=10)) # Output: 2

Snippet 1. Modular Division with Rounding

One Size at a Time

We have seen that, after rescaling by a factor \(p\), the ciphertext moves from a modulus \(q\) to a smaller modulus \(q'\). However, the factor \(p\) is not chosen at random. The initial modulus, in fact, is built as the product of several factors:

\[ q^{(L)}=p_L\cdot p_{L-1}\cdots p_1\cdot p_0 \]

Formula 3. Modulus Construction

The ciphertext therefore starts from modulus \(q^{(L)}\), the largest available. Note that \(L\) indicates a level, not a numerical value. We will see this later, but first let us perform a rescaling and eliminate one of the factors, for example \(p_L\). Let us see what happens to the modulus:

\[ q^{(L-1)} = \frac{q^{(L)}}{p_L} = p_{L-1}\cdots p_1\cdot p_0 \]

If we perform another rescaling and eliminate another factor, for example \(p_{L-1}\), we obtain

\[ q^{(L-2)} = \frac{q^{(L - 1)}}{p_{L - 1}} = p_{L-2}\cdots p_1\cdot p_0 \]

and so on. We thus obtain a sequence of progressively smaller moduli:

\[ q^{(L)} \longrightarrow q^{(L-1)} \longrightarrow \cdots \longrightarrow q^{(0)} \]

Formula 4. Modulus Chain

This sequence is called the modulus chain. Each element of the chain represents a level at which the ciphertext can be. Let us look at an example. Suppose we build the initial modulus as

\[ q^{(3)}=11 \cdot 13 \cdot 17 \cdot 19= 46\,189 \]

The level is \(3\) because we build the modulus as the product of \(4\) factors (counting starts at \(0\), this is not MATLAB). If the first rescaling eliminates factor \(19\), we move to

\[ q^{(2)} = \frac{46\,189}{19} = 11 \cdot 13 \cdot 17 = 2\,431 \]

then, eliminating \(17\), we obtain

\[ q^{(1)} = \frac{2\,431}{17} = 11 \cdot 13 = 143 \]

finally, eliminating \(13\), there remains

\[ q^{(0)} = \frac{143}{13} = 11 \]

The modulus chain is therefore:

\[ q^{(3)} = 46\,189 \longrightarrow q^{(2)} = 2\,431 \longrightarrow q^{(1)} = 143 \longrightarrow q^{(0)} = 11 \]

For the modulus chain as well, here is a small explanatory snippet:

from math import prod

class ModulusChain:

    def __init__(self, *factors: int):
        self._factors = tuple(factors)
        self._moduli = self._build_moduli(self._factors)

    def modulus_at(self, level: int) -> int:
        return self._moduli[level]

    def next_modulus_at(self, level: int) -> int:
        return self._moduli[level + 1]

    def rescaling_factor_at(self, level: int) -> int:
        current_modulus = self.modulus_at(level)
        next_modulus = self.next_modulus_at(level)

        return current_modulus // next_modulus

    @staticmethod
    def _build_moduli(factors: tuple[int, ...]) -> tuple[int, ...]:
        return tuple(
            prod(factors[:remaining])
            for remaining in range(len(factors), 0, -1)
        )

# 3 Levels Chain
chain = ModulusChain(1003, 1009, 1013)

# Level 0
print(chain.modulus_at(0)) # Output: 1003 * 1009 * 1013 = 1025183351
print(chain.next_modulus_at(0)) # Output: 1003 * 1009 = 1012027
print(chain.rescaling_factor_at(0)) # Output: 1025183351 // 1012027 = 1013

# Level 1
print(chain.modulus_at(1)) # Output: 1003 * 1009 = 1012027
print(chain.next_modulus_at(1)) # Output: 1003
print(chain.rescaling_factor_at(1)) # Output: 1012027 // 1003 = 1009

Snippet 2. Modulus Chain

After each rescaling, the ciphertext goes down one level. The scale becomes manageable again, but the factor removed from the modulus cannot be recovered. In practice, to reduce the size of the scale, we consume part of the space we had initially prepared. This means that we cannot rescale indefinitely. Once we reach the last level of the chain, there are no other factors left to eliminate. Since it is primarily multiplications that cause the scale to grow and require rescaling, the number of available levels limits the number of consecutive multiplications we can perform. This limit is called multiplicative depth. The modulus chain must therefore be prepared taking into account the computation we want to perform. If we choose few levels, we will consume them quickly. If we choose too many, we will have to start from a much larger initial modulus, but we will see the consequences of this choice when we talk about security.

For the moment, it is enough to know that every ciphertext will have two pieces of information to carry along: its scale and the level of the chain in which it is found.

As for part 2, I tried to develop some code with a minimum of organization. In the repo, you will find three classes:

  • ModulusChain: represents the modulus chain and provides methods to access moduli and rescaling factors
  • Level: represents a level of the modulus chain and makes it possible to identify the current and next level.
  • Rescaler: handles rescaling the coefficients of a polynomial, dividing them by the factor associated with the current level and moving to the next modulus.

Conclusions

We have reached the end of this third part of the series on homomorphic encryption. This time as well, we have not encrypted anything yet, but with rescaling we have solved a problem that would have become rather annoying later on. So now we know that size matters, but we have also learned how to manage it. In short, we have learned to keep size under control, but we still have a rather obvious problem: up to this point, our messages are still completely in the clear.
